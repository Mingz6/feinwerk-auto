Your Mercedes-Benz tells you when it wants a service, and it names it with a letter: Service A or Service B. Here's what each one usually includes, when the car asks for it, and what you can say no to.

## How the car decides

Most Mercedes-Benz cars sold in Canada since the late 2000s use a flexible service schedule. The instrument cluster counts down time and distance and shows "Service A" or "Service B" when one is due. In North America the usual rhythm is a service about once a year or every 16,000 km, whichever comes first, alternating between A and B. Your owner's manual or service booklet has the exact schedule for your model and year, and that booklet wins over anything written here.

Some cars show a letter with a number, such as B3. The number adds work for that visit, for example spark plugs or a transmission service. We read the exact list for your car before we quote.

## Service A

The smaller one:

- Engine oil and filter, with an oil that carries the Mercedes-Benz approval for your engine (the number on the bottle, such as MB 229.5)
- Fluid levels checked and topped up
- Tire pressures checked and set, tires looked over
- Brakes inspected: pad thickness and discs
- Service indicator reset

## Service B

Everything in Service A, plus:

- Cabin air filter replaced
- Brake fluid replaced, if it's due by time (usually every two years)
- A wider inspection: steering and suspension parts, exhaust, belts, underbody, lights

## What you can skip, and what you can't

Nothing in A or B is padding. Oil, filters and brake fluid age whether you drive or not.

What we'd question are extras added to the same invoice: fuel system cleaning, engine flushes, or nitrogen in the tires. None of those is on the maker's schedule. If a shop recommends one, ask what measurement or symptom says your car needs it.

## Does it have to be the brand's own service centre?

For routine maintenance, the work matters more than the building. Whoever does it should use parts and oil that meet the maker's specification and give you an itemized invoice with the part numbers and oil approval. Keep every invoice. If your car is still under the new-car warranty, check the warranty booklet for what records it asks you to keep.
